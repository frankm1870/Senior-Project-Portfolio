Mycelium Composites


  • Overview

    For this research project, I decided to investigate the mechanical properties of mycelium composites and how they vary with the substrate used to make them. My preliminary research showed that the substrate has a large effect on density and mechanical properties, and the type of mycelium has less of an effect on mechanical properties, and no effect on thermal properties (Bayomei et al. 2026). I will also test the electrical conductivity of mycelium composites to test their effect as a renewable insulator.

  • Global Importance

    Mycelium composites, being composed of mycelium and a lignocellulosic substrate, are extremely biodegradable, making them a much more sustainable material than alternatives such as plastic. Additionally, their growth is a fairly simple process, which means the only barrier to their use is their weak mechanical properties. Already, however, there are proposed uses of them as insulation material in homes (Attias et al., 2019). If their mechanical properties could be improved, however, they could become an extremely common material, possibly replacing plastics.

  • Personal Importance

    I believe that mycelium composites are interesting due to their potential ability to replace plastics. For so long, plastics have been the most commonly used material, yet also the biggest sustainability problem. Something that could replace them in many uses would likely be the biggest materials science revolution yet. As such, I want to find ways to improve their strength so that they can be used in more scenarios.

Research Plan


  • Research Question

    How does the substrate used for growing mycelium affect the mechanical properties of the resulting composite material?

  • Background

    Mycelium composites, also called mycelium biocomposites or MBCs, are a composite material made of a substrate (typically lignocellulosic waste such as sawdust or grains) with mycelium grown into it. The strength of the material comes from hyphae slowly decomposing the substrate to form small voids that they bind onto (Chen et al. 2026). This results in the substrate being tightly bound together by a matrix of hyphae. This matrix primarily consists of chitin, which acts as an adhesive, binding the composite together. There are three different types of hyphae branching structures: generative, skeletal, and binding (Anik et al. 2026). Generative hyphae are highly branched and are responsible for spore production. Skeletal hyphae and primarily unbranched and greatly increase material strength. Binding hyphae are also highly branched, however they primarily form cross-links between other hyphae, further strengthening the material.

  • Hypothesis

    Cellulose, although containing less accessible sugars than coffee grinds, is expected to create the strongest composite, as the slower breakdown of cellulose means that more substrate will remain for mycelium to bind together. Between sawdust and coffee grinds, coffee grinds are expected to create the next strongest, as they provide a large amount of nutrients for mycelium to grow while retraining some particles for mycelium to attach to. Sawdust is likely to produce the weakest composite, as the presence of lignin surrounding the sugars is likely to slow down the decomposition process.

  • Materials and Methods

    The composites were made using 17.5cm3 of each substrate type, placed into 3 sets of the shaped molds shown in Fig. 1. Each substrate was first mixed with water in a beaker to saturation, ensuring that all unabsorbed water was removed through filtration. The substrates were then mixed with a mass of Ganoderma lucidum equal to 1% of their original mass. The molds were sterilized by soaking them in bleach before being rinsed and dried. The substrates were then placed in a hot water bath to sterilize them, before they were then put into the molds. The samples were left to grow for 14 days in a dark environment inside of separate bags.
